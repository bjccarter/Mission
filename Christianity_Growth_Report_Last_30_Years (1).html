The Growth of Christianity by Country

A Detailed Report on the Last 30 Years (1996–2026)

Global Shifts, Regional Transformations, and Biblical Reflections

Author: Ben Carter

Researcher & Writer on Global Christianity

Executive Summary

Over the past three decades, Christianity has undergone a profound geographic and demographic transformation. While absolute numbers of Christians have continued to grow—reaching approximately 2.6 billion by 2025—the center of gravity has decisively shifted from the Global North (Europe and North America) to the Global South, particularly Sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Asia and Latin America.

Key drivers include high fertility rates and strong retention in Africa, the rise of Pentecostal and Charismatic movements, and significant (though sometimes contested) growth in Asia. In contrast, Europe and North America have seen declining percentages due to secularization and religious disaffiliation, even as absolute numbers remain large in some countries.

Global Trends: 1996–2026

From roughly 1.9–2.0 billion Christians around the year 2000, the global Christian population has grown to an estimated 2.6 billion in 2025. This growth has been slower than overall world population growth in recent decades, leading to a slight decline in Christianity’s share of the global population (from about 32–33% to around 29–32% depending on the source and year).

According to the Center for the Study of Global Christianity (Gordon-Conwell), Africa has been the fastest-growing region at approximately 2.6% annually in recent years, followed by Asia. Europe has experienced slight absolute declines.

Regional Transformation

Sub-Saharan Africa: The New Center

Sub-Saharan Africa surpassed Europe as the region with the largest number of Christians during the 2010s. By 2020, it was home to about 697 million Christians (Pew), or roughly 31% of all Christians worldwide—up dramatically from just 1% in 1910 and a much smaller share in the 1990s.

  • High birth rates and strong intergenerational retention
  • Growth of independent, Pentecostal, and Charismatic churches
  • Countries like Nigeria, DR Congo, Ethiopia, Kenya, and Tanzania leading absolute growth

Asia and Latin America

Asia has seen notable growth, particularly in house churches in China (estimates range from ~25–100+ million depending on source), India, and Southeast Asia. Latin America remains overwhelmingly Christian (~85%), but has experienced a major internal shift from Catholicism toward Protestant, Pentecostal, and evangelical expressions, alongside some rise in the religiously unaffiliated.

Areas of Rapid Growth

Many of the fastest percentage growth rates in recent decades have occurred in countries where Christianity was a small minority, including parts of Asia (Nepal, China historically, Mongolia, Cambodia) and some African nations. Absolute growth is concentrated in high-fertility African countries and populous nations like Nigeria and the Philippines.

Spotlight: Iran – Explosive Underground Growth

Iran is one of the most remarkable stories of Christian growth in the past 30–45 years. Official census figures count only recognized ethnic Christians (mainly Armenians and Assyrians/Chaldeans) at roughly 120,000–130,000. Converts from Islam are not counted and remain legally Muslim.

Broader estimates that include underground Muslim-background believers (MBBs) range from about 500,000–800,000 (Open Doors, Article 18, World Religion Database) up to 1–2 million or higher according to ministries active inside the country (Elam, Iran Alive, Heart4Iran). From only a few hundred MBBs in 1979, the growth has been extraordinary.

Drivers of Growth

  • Widespread disillusionment with the Islamic Republic’s version of Shia Islam, especially among youth
  • Frequent reports of dreams and visions of Jesus
  • Satellite TV, internet, and online training programs
  • Decentralized indigenous house-church networks
  • Persecution that has often accelerated rather than stopped the movement

Despite severe persecution (Iran ranks in the top 10 of Open Doors’ World Watch List), the underground church continues to expand through small house groups and digital means.

Spotlight: India – Steady Growth and House-Church Movement

India’s official 2011 Census recorded approximately 27.8 million Christians (2.3% of the population). The Christian share has remained remarkably stable between roughly 2% and 3% since 1951, while absolute numbers have grown with the overall population (from about 19.5 million in 1991 to 28 million in 2011).

Some Christian demographic sources and ministries report higher figures (sometimes 4–5% or tens of millions more), including “hidden” believers or house-church members who may not identify as Christian in the census due to social pressure, loss of caste-based benefits, or fear of anti-conversion laws.

Geographic Concentration

  • Strong majorities in several Northeast states (Nagaland, Mizoram, Meghalaya)
  • Significant populations in Kerala, Goa, and parts of South India
  • Growing house-church movements especially in North India (Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, etc.)

Current Dynamics

  • Anti-conversion laws in many states
  • Rising reports of persecution under Hindu nationalist pressures
  • Active indigenous house-church planting and discipleship among marginalized communities
  • Growth often among tribal and lower-caste groups

Unlike Iran’s dramatic conversion-driven explosion from a near-zero base, India’s story is one of long-term stability in percentage combined with absolute growth and a vibrant, often under-the-radar house-church movement in the face of legal and social restrictions.

Declines in the West and Internal Shifts

Between 2010 and 2020 alone, the Christian share of the population declined by 5 or more percentage points in 41 countries, nearly all of them in Europe, the Americas, and Australia/New Zealand. Notable examples include Australia (−20 points), Chile (−18), Uruguay (−16), and the United States and Canada (−14 each). Several countries lost their Christian majority status (UK, Australia, France, Uruguay).

In Latin America, while overall Christian identification remains high, there has been a marked shift: Catholicism has declined as a share while evangelical and Pentecostal churches have grown substantially since the mid-1990s.

Denominational Dynamics

Within global Christianity, the most dynamic growth has been among Pentecostals, Charismatics, and Evangelicals. Catholics remain the largest single tradition (approximately 1.3 billion), but their growth rate has been slower than that of Protestants and Independents. Pentecostal/Charismatic Christians number well over 600 million and have been among the fastest-growing segments for decades.

Bible Study Discussion Points

1. The Great Commission in Action (Matthew 28:18–20)

“Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you. And behold, I am with you always, to the end of the age.”

Discussion: How do the dramatic shifts toward the Global South reflect the “all nations” scope of the Commission? What does it mean that the largest Christian populations are now in Africa rather than Europe? How should churches in the Global North respond to this reality?

2. Growth Amid Persecution and Difficulty (Acts 8:1–4; 2 Timothy 2:8–10)

“And there arose on that day a great persecution against the church in Jerusalem, and they were all scattered... Now those who were scattered went about preaching the word.”

Discussion: In several places where Christianity has grown rapidly (parts of China, Iran, Nigeria, etc.), believers have faced significant opposition. How does the book of Acts show that pressure can fuel rather than extinguish the spread of the gospel? What practical lessons does this offer for churches today?

3. A Multitude from Every Nation (Revelation 7:9–10)

“After this I looked, and behold, a great multitude that no one could number, from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages, standing before the throne and before the Lamb...”

Discussion: The demographic center of Christianity moving south and east brings the church closer to the diversity envisioned in Revelation. What opportunities and challenges does this present for global Christian unity, theology, and mission? How can local churches better reflect this coming reality?

4. Faithfulness in Decline (2 Timothy 4:1–5; Hebrews 10:23–25)

“Preach the word; be ready in season and out of season... For the time is coming when people will not endure sound teaching...”

Discussion: In Europe, North America, and parts of Latin America, percentages are declining due to disaffiliation. How should Christians respond to secularization—through renewed discipleship, cultural engagement, or something else? What does biblical faithfulness look like when numbers are shrinking locally but growing globally?

5. The Power of the Spirit and Indigenous Expression (Acts 2; 1 Corinthians 12)

Discussion: Much of the growth in Africa, Asia, and Latin America has occurred through Pentecostal and Charismatic movements emphasizing the work of the Holy Spirit, healing, and local leadership. How does this align with (or challenge) traditional understandings of the church’s expansion? What can the wider church learn from these expressions?

Conclusion

The story of Christianity over the last 30 years is one of both continuity and dramatic change. The faith continues to grow in absolute numbers and remains the world’s largest religion, but its demographic and geographic profile has been fundamentally reshaped. The Global South is no longer the future of the church—it is the present. Understanding these shifts is essential for mission, theology, and the self-understanding of Christians everywhere.

While statistics tell an important story, they are secondary to the deeper reality of people coming to faith, communities being transformed, and the ongoing work of God across cultures and continents.

Personal Application: How This Should Affect You as a Christian

The demographic realities in this report are not merely interesting statistics. They should reshape how we pray, prioritize, and live as followers of Jesus.

1. Adjust Your Mental Map of the Church

The center of gravity of global Christianity has shifted. Most Christians now live in the Global South (Africa, Asia, Latin America). The church is no longer primarily “Western.”

Response

  • Stop thinking of the “typical Christian” as European or North American.
  • Learn from and pray for churches in Nigeria, Ethiopia, China, India, Iran, and the Philippines with the same seriousness you give to churches in your own country.
  • Read Scripture with the awareness that the majority of its readers today are not from the historic West.

2. Let the Great Commission Feel Concrete (Matthew 28:18–20)

The report shows the gospel advancing in places that were once considered very resistant (Iran is the clearest example). It also shows large populations still largely unreached or only lightly reached.

Response

  • Pray regularly for specific countries and peoples highlighted in the report (Iran’s underground church, North India’s house churches, the growth in Sub-Saharan Africa, the decline in the West).
  • Ask: “What is my part?” — prayer, giving, going, welcoming, or sending.
  • Resist the idea that mission is finished or only for professionals.

3. Expect and Prepare for Persecution (Acts 8; 2 Timothy 3:12)

Growth in Iran, China, and parts of India is happening under real pressure. The report shows that opposition has often accompanied, not stopped, expansion.

Response

  • Pray for persecuted believers by name and country (Open Doors and similar lists are useful).
  • Strengthen your own discipleship so that comfort is not the goal of your faith.
  • Support churches and ministries that work with persecuted believers without sensationalizing their suffering.

4. Hold Both Joy and Lament

There is real reason for joy: absolute numbers of Christians are still rising, and remarkable movements are occurring. There is also reason for sober reflection: the Christian percentage is declining in much of Europe, North America, and parts of Latin America through disaffiliation.

Response

  • Rejoice in what God is doing in the Global South and in underground churches.
  • Lament secularization and nominalism where you live, and ask what repentance and renewal look like in your own context.
  • Avoid both triumphalism (“we’re winning everywhere”) and despair (“the church is dying”).

5. Practical Next Steps You Can Take This Week

  • Prayer: Choose one country or region from the report (Iran, India, Nigeria, or your own country) and pray for it daily for a month.
  • Learning: Read one book or series of articles by a non-Western Christian leader.
  • Local church: Ask how your congregation is connected to the global church (missions, partnerships, immigrant believers in your city).
  • Generosity: Direct some giving toward gospel work in the places of rapid growth or high persecution.
  • Personal faithfulness: The demographic shifts do not change the call to love your neighbor, make disciples, and remain faithful where you are.

The Bible study discussion points earlier in this report (Great Commission, growth amid persecution, the multi-ethnic vision of Revelation 7:9) are meant for exactly this kind of personal application. Sit with one of them this week and ask: “What should change in my prayers, priorities, or perspective because of this?”

Loving the Lost: A Biblical Response to Global Realities

The numbers in this report are not abstract. Behind every percentage and population figure are real people—many of whom have never clearly heard the gospel. Growth in some places and decline in others should deepen our love for the lost, not produce pride or indifference.

Scripture Foundation

“For the Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost.” — Luke 19:10

“The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise as some count slowness, but is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach repentance.” — 2 Peter 3:9

“Go therefore and make disciples of all nations…” — Matthew 28:19

How the Data Should Shape Our Love

  • Iran and restricted nations: Rapid underground growth reminds us that God is at work even where opposition is severe. Love looks like persistent prayer and support for those risking everything to follow Jesus and reach others.
  • India and South Asia: Large populations with relatively small Christian percentages call for patient, culturally sensitive witness and discipleship, especially among unreached and marginalized groups.
  • Sub-Saharan Africa: Explosive growth is cause for thanksgiving, yet it also calls for depth—loving the lost means helping new believers become mature disciples who can reach still more.
  • The West: Declining percentages mean many nominal or former Christians, plus growing numbers of the religiously unaffiliated, live near us. Love begins with our neighbors, coworkers, and family members.

Practical Expressions of Loving the Lost

  • Pray specifically and regularly for unreached peoples and for revival where the church is declining.
  • Share the gospel clearly and graciously with people in your own community.
  • Support missionaries, church planters, and indigenous workers in places of growth and persecution.
  • Welcome and disciple immigrants and international students who may be more open to the gospel away from home.
  • Live in such a way that your life makes the gospel attractive (Titus 2:10; 1 Peter 3:15).

The growth of the church is ultimately God’s work. Our part is to love the lost the way Jesus does—patiently, sacrificially, and with urgency—until every nation has heard.
